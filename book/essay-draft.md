I have read a lot. A lot about technology, design, and how we consider the ethics of design out in the world. I also like to make things. Why not make something about all the books that I have read (or at least intend to read!).

<!-- NEW: from "Waste = Food", "Why reading" and "The unread". -->
Books are perfect objects. You buy one, do things with it, and pass it on, and its ideas keep finding new audiences. Even an old book can become incredibly useful in a new context. Reading is still a great way to take in information, and keeping books is a way to collect things and "prove" they're important. I haven't read every book here. You can't read everything. It still helps to have them, and to know more or less what is in them.

Every spine is drawn at {scale} of its real size, from two numbers: how tall the book is, and how thick. I found the height of {height_known}. The other {height_guessed} heights are guesses (I will be measuring them as time allows). Thickness is harder to find. Only {thick_known} came with one. For {thick_from_pages} more, a short rule worked it out from the page count. The other {thick_guessed} are guesses, and a rule made those too.

After that, I still didn’t draw any of these spines. Nor did I work at all with the traditional software tools for page layout — Illustrator, InDesign, Affinity, Inkscape. The catalogue predominantly lives in a database and some template files. A script turns it into a website, library.wjerk.shop, and the same data feeds a different folder of html and css and python files and becomes this book. Another script lines up the spines, starts a new stretch of shelf when one fills, and cuts a title back to its last whole word when it won’t fit. I wrote the rules to my vision, and the rules make the pages. When a page looks wrong, I change the rules to improve it.

A design system is a set of written-down decisions: these colors, this type, this space usage. I have been trying to build a much clearer personal design system to not have to make the same decision twice. This way a script, a student, or even an AI can make a decision the way I would. It holds my taste, so I can spend my attention (hopefully) more wisely. And more rapidly get to the "normal" way that I would start a design problem.

The tools are open. Thirteen typefaces set the spines, all under the SIL Open Font License, which lets anyone use, change and share them. The software that set them is open source. The text is Creative Commons. The catalogue is a public file anyone can download.

moves here, where it has company. -->
I always try to find out who designed a book. The ones I go back to carry their ideas in the object itself: *The Medium Is the Massage* and the Jerome Agel books after it; *Climates*, where the paper gets thicker as the book goes on, like rising seas; *Eternally Yours*, with one thing going on in the left-hand pages and something else in the right. The design of this book is a minor homage to *I Seem to Be a Verb* by Buckminster Fuller and Jerome Agel: one line of type runs through every spread.

<!-- NEW: from "The book I'd save in a fire". -->
The book I have referenced most in my design life is Stewart Brand’s *How Buildings Learn*, even though it is an annoyingly landscape 8.5 by 11. Buildings keep changing after they are built, and the ordinary ones, the ones regular people adapt without a designer, learn best. This book works the same way. The catalogue keeps changing, and the book is rebuilt from it. The date on the title page is just the last time I ran it.

<!-- NEW: from the ending notes. -->
Two of the spines on these shelves are records: Brian Eno’s *Discreet Music* and *Music for Airports*. Eno set up tape loops, started them, and let the system make the music. Sol LeWitt said the idea becomes a machine that makes the art. This book is a small machine like those, and the machine is easier to build now. Most of the code for it came out of a conversation with an AI coding assistant.

<!-- NEW: from the ending notes, the questions left as questions. -->
So where do we keep the organic systems that come with designing for people, and for the earth? How do these tools meet our normal equipment and spaces? The copies of this book come off a risograph, onto the blank backs of old paper from my office, and I fold each leaf by hand.

Books are good. I still believe in reading.
